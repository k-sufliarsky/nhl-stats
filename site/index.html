How the rating works, and where the data comes from

Skaters get a Game Score, the single-game metric published by Dom Luszczyszyn: 0.75 × goals + 0.70 × primary assists + 0.55 × secondary assists + 0.075 × shots on goal + 0.05 × blocked shots + 0.15 × penalties drawn − 0.15 × penalties taken + 0.01 × faceoffs won − 0.01 × faceoffs lost + 0.05 × 5-on-5 shot attempts for on ice − 0.05 × against + 0.15 × goals for on ice − 0.15 × goals against on ice.

Goalies are rated on goals saved above expected: the expected goals of every unblocked shot they faced minus the goals they allowed. Expected goals come from a model fitted on about 25,000 unblocked shots from every NHL game this season, using shot distance, angle and whether it was a rebound or off a turnover.

From score to 0–10: each raw score is ranked against every NHL forward, defenseman or goalie game this season, and that rank is placed on a bell curve centred on 5. Ranking first matters because Game Score is lopsided: most games are quiet and a few are huge, and a straight scale sent about one forward game in sixty to a perfect 10. Now 7 means better than about 91% of games, 8 better than 98%, 9 better than 99.6%, and 10 is reserved for the very best few games of the season. A goal and an assist usually lands around 7.3, two goals around 7.7, a hat trick around 8.8.

Limits: ratings reward what the scoresheet and on-ice shot counts can see. Defensive positioning, zone exits and passing that never reaches the scoresheet are invisible to it, so treat one game's number as a summary, not a verdict.
